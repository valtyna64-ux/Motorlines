Maverick '69 — Ten Years to Make It Roar Again

ML — An inheritance, a promise, and ten years to bring it back to life.

October 4, 2026 | 9:00 pm

PHOTO: JUAN

Property of Juan. Photograph taken by the owner and provided for this article.

“It’s a ’69 Maverick. An uncle, my father’s brother, had it from new, but he crashed it and the entire front end was destroyed. It remained in his garage without being repaired. I always asked my uncle to fix it together, but for different reasons, we never did. He passed away from diabetes, and to my surprise, he had left the car to me in his will. When I turned 25, I began the restoration, and ten years later, here is the result. It runs like a demon. I love taking it out on the highway with my favorite songs playing (the stereo is the original cassette player). Sometimes tango, Balkan music, pasodoble... I’m very diverse when it comes to music.” — Juan, owner of the 1969 Ford Maverick.

The Art of Restoration

For years, many classic cars have ended up abandoned in garages, yards, or workshops, waiting for a second chance. Restoring them requires time, patience, and above all, a special connection with the machine. Juan’s ’69 Maverick is precisely that: a car that went from being abandoned to becoming the star of the road once again. But bringing it back to life also meant making significant modifications. Juan tells us that the entire front end had to be replaced with one from another car, since the original had been beyond repair after the accident. Among the modifications he made are a 2.0L turbocharged EcoBoost gasoline engine and a 2.5L hybrid system, along with leather interiors and a black paint finish.

Despite all these modifications, there was one detail he decided to preserve, one that directly connects Perla Negra with its history: the original cassette stereo. A small element that remains part of this Maverick’s personality and of the stories Juan continues to create behind the wheel.

How Much Does It Cost to Restore a Classic?

The cost can increase rapidly depending on the condition of the bodywork, engine, interior, paint, and availability of spare parts. Added to this are the hours of labor and the number of years it can take to complete a project.

Its owner decided to name it “Perla Negra,” a name that perfectly matches the personality this Maverick has developed over the years. More than just a car, it has become his companion through adventures, road trips, and countless stories on the highway, including sharing some of those moments with girls. With the attitude of a true rockstar, Perla Negra has one particular rule: its owner does not usually lend it out, and he is the only one who drives it. After so many miles, adventures, and memories, it is not simply a car to him, but a machine that has become part of his own story.

Early Mavericks

In 1969, the six-cylinder 170 cubic-inch inline-six was available, equivalent to approximately 2.8 liters, paired with either a three-speed manual or three-speed automatic transmission, depending on the configuration.

(Automobile Catalog).

The name “Maverick” was not chosen at random.

The word Maverick originally referred to an unbranded animal or one without an identified owner. Ford accompanied the name with an emblem inspired by the head of a Texas Longhorn. (Ford Mexico, “History of the Ford Maverick.”)

The earliest 1969 Mavericks have details that set them apart.

The earliest examples had the ignition switch located on the dashboard. Vehicles built after September 1, 1969 incorporated the ignition switch into a locking steering column, as part of the changes required by new federal safety regulations for 1970 models. (Ford Maverick — historical documentation)

The Maverick survived long beyond 1969.

North American production continued until 1977, and throughout its production run, approximately 2.1 million units were built, making it one of Ford’s most successful compact cars of the 1970s. (Classic Industries, 1970–1977 Ford Maverick: History)

The design was inspired by the Mustang.

Its long hood, fastback-style roof, and short rear end followed the visual language that Ford had popularized with the Mustang. (Ford Mexico, “Ford Maverick: History of a Rebel”)

Its original price was only US$1,995.

One of the Maverick’s main attractions was precisely its price. In 1969, Ford aimed to offer a new car for less than $2,000, which was a fundamental part of the project’s strategy. (Business: The Making of the Maverick)
